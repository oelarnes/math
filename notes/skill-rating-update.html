One skill rating update

Technical note for 17Lands developers · October 2026 · companion to the skill groups proposal

This note writes out how the skill-group rating model updates one player's rating from one set-format of results, with every model parameter held fixed. Skill is the log-odds of winning a game against a standardized general Premier Draft opponent played at platinum rank, relative to a per-set baseline. The update is a scalar Kalman filter with a Laplace (Gaussian) approximation to the binomial game likelihood, as in Glicko. It also splits the player's surprise among four sources: a change in core skill, a set-specific deviation, deck quality, and game luck. Section 7 covers how skill groups are assigned from the ratings, and Section 8 works through a real update: one player's Outlaws of Thunder Junction Premier Draft record.

1. Quantities

Skill is the log-odds of winning a game against a standardized general Premier Draft opponent played at platinum rank, relative to a per-set baseline. Log-odds and win rate convert through the logit function and its inverse:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p) = \log\frac{p}{1-p}, \qquad \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} . $$

In 17Lands summaries the rank bucket is the player's own rank at the time of the game: opponent rank is no longer in the logs, and the summary generator falls back to the player's rank.

2. Model

A game in draft \(d\) of set-format \(t\), played at rank \(r\), is won with probability

$$ \Pr(\text{win}) = \operatorname{logit}^{-1}\!\big(s_t + a_t + e_{t,d} - o_r\big), \qquad o_r = b_t + \beta_r , $$

with independent \(a_t \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_a^2)\) and \(e_{t,d} \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau^2)\). Core skill follows a random walk in calendar time, \(s_{t} = s_{t-1} + \eta_t\) with \(\eta_t \sim \mathcal N(0,\, c^2 \Delta_t)\), where \(\Delta_t\) is the time between the two set-formats in years. Each set-format is dated at the midpoint of its expansion's original release window, from its release until the next release. Events from later reruns count toward the original set-format. A player seen for the first time starts at \(s \sim \mathcal N(\mu_0, \sigma^2)\).

The update needs one simplification. The deck terms enter at the level of the whole set-format, as their average \(\bar e_t = \frac1D \sum_d e_{t,d} \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau^2/D)\). Summary rows record total games and wins rather than each draft's record, so this is the natural granularity.

3. Step 1: carry the rating forward

Between set-formats the rating mean stays where it is, and its variance grows by the drift accumulated over the gap, up to a cap:

$$ m_t^- = m_{t-1}, \qquad P_t^- = \min\!\big(P_{t-1} + c^2 \Delta_t,\ \sigma^2\big). $$

This is the Kalman prediction step for a random walk (Glickman 1999 does the same for Glicko's rating deviation). The cap is a rule about our belief, not part of the generative model. It stops a long-absent player's uncertainty from exceeding a newcomer's. Their old rating is kept, but it carries no more confidence than the population prior. Skill groups for a new set-format are assigned at this point, from the carried-forward ratings (Section 7).

4. Step 2: how well a skill value explains the record

Fix the effective skill \(x\). Each game at rank \(r\) is won independently with probability \(p_r = \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(x - o_r)\), so the log-likelihood of the record is built up in four steps:

$$ \begin{aligned} \Pr[\,w_r \text{ wins in } n_r \text{ games}\,] &= \binom{n_r}{w_r}\, p_r^{\,w_r} (1 - p_r)^{\,n_r - w_r} \\ L(x) &= \prod_r \binom{n_r}{w_r}\, p_r^{\,w_r} (1 - p_r)^{\,n_r - w_r} \\ \ell(x) = \log L(x) &= \sum_r \Big[ w_r \log p_r + (n_r - w_r) \log (1 - p_r) \Big] + \text{const} \\ &= \sum_r \Big[ w_r \log \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(x - o_r) + (n_r - w_r) \log \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(o_r - x) \Big] + \text{const} \end{aligned} $$
  1. Within one rank bucket the wins are binomial.
  2. Buckets are independent given \(x\), so the likelihood is the product over buckets.
  3. Taking logs turns the product into a sum. The binomial coefficients don't involve \(x\), so they fold into a constant.
  4. Substitute \(p_r\) and use \(1 - \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(z) = \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(-z)\).

The last line is the log-likelihood of a logistic regression with one coefficient, \(x\), and a fixed offset \(o_r\) per bucket. Premier Draft's stopping rule (7 wins or 3 losses) changes only the constant. The rule depends on results alone, so any particular sequence of games still has probability equal to a product of \(p_r\) and \(1 - p_r\) terms (the likelihood principle).

Higher \(\ell(x)\) means \(x\) explains the record better. On its own it would pick the skill that best fits this one set-format, ignoring everything known about the player before.

5. Step 3: the update

What is estimated

One update estimates a single quantity per player: core skill \(s_t\), reported as a rating \(m_t\) with variance \(P_t\). Everything else is fixed going in:

  • From the player's history: the carried-forward rating \(m_t^-\) and its variance \(P_t^-\) (Step 1). They say where the player was, and how sure we are, after drift.
  • From this set-format: the record \((w_r, n_r)\), the number of drafts \(D\), and the benchmark \(o_r\). Together they give \(\ell\) from Section 4.
  • Shared parameters: \(\sigma_a\) and \(\tau\), which say how much of a set's result can be unrelated to core skill.

The cost function

The new rating is the skill value that minimizes a cost with two terms:

$$ \text{cost}(s) = \underbrace{-\,w\,\ell(s)}_{\text{misfit to this set's record}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{(s - m_t^-)^2}{2P_t^-}}_{\text{distance from the carried-forward rating}}, \qquad w = \frac{1}{1 + I\,(\sigma_a^2 + \tau^2/D)} . $$
  • The first term is the logistic loss of Section 4. It pulls the rating toward the skill that best explains this set's games.
  • The second term is a ridge penalty centred on the previous rating. Its strength \(1/P_t^-\) comes from the player's history: a long, consistent history makes it stiff, and time away loosens it.
  • The weight \(w\) discounts the record for set deviation and deck noise. Here \(I = \sum_r n_r p_r (1 - p_r)\) is the record's information, roughly a quarter of the number of games. A few games are weighted almost fully. A large record is discounted, because more games can't average away the set deviation: one set-format is one draw of it. However many games are played, the record counts for at most \(1/(\sigma_a^2 + \tau^2/D)\).

The rating's variance is read off the shape of the cost at its minimum: \(P_t\) is one over the curvature there. A sharply curved minimum means a confident rating.

How it is solved

The cost has one minimum, found with a few Newton steps from the carried-forward rating. Appendix A shows the algebra. The steps are usually written in Kalman-filter form, which is what the code and the worked example use. Each step condenses the record into the skill it points to, \(y\), and that point's noise variance, \(R\):

$$ y = \hat x + \frac{U}{I}, \qquad R = \underbrace{\frac{1}{I}}_{\text{luck}} + \underbrace{\frac{\tau^2}{D}}_{\text{deck}} + \underbrace{\sigma_a^2}_{\text{set}}, \qquad U = \sum_r \big(w_r - n_r p_r\big), $$

with \(p_r\) evaluated at the current estimate \(\hat x\). The rating then moves part of the way from the prior toward \(y\):

$$ S = P_t^- + R, \qquad K = \frac{P_t^-}{S}, \qquad m_t = m_t^- + K\,(y - m_t^-), \qquad P_t = (1 - K)\,P_t^- . $$

Repeating with \(\hat x = m_t\) converges in about two steps. The code runs three.

\(K\) is the share of the surprise \(y - m_t^-\) that moves the rating. Early in a player's history \(P_t^-\) is large, so the rating moves most of the way to the new result. For an established player \(P_t^-\) has shrunk below \(\sigma_a^2\), and most of a surprise is read as "this set", not "this player changed". Drift sets how low \(P\) can go, and so how far back the rating effectively remembers.

Fitting the shared parameters

The update takes \(c\), \(\sigma_a\), \(\tau\), \(\sigma\), \(\mu_0\) and the rank offsets as given. They are fitted once, across all players, by how well the model predicts each set-format's record before seeing it. For every player-set, the model assigns a probability to the actual record using only the carried-forward rating. The fit chooses the parameters that make those records, together, most probable. Each probability averages the binomial record over the rating's uncertainty, computed numerically (adaptive Gauss–Hermite quadrature, Liu and Pierce 1994), and a standard optimizer searches the parameters. Too little drift, say, makes returning players' results look surprising; too much makes every rating vague. The fit balances the two.

6. Step 4: attributing the surprise

The skill the record points to, \(y\), is core skill plus three things that aren't: the set deviation, the drafts' average deck quality, and game luck \(\varepsilon\), whose variance is \(1/I\). So the surprise has four parts, \(y - m_t^- = (s_t - m_t^-) + a_t + \bar e_t + \varepsilon\). Given the prior they are independent zero-mean Gaussians, each with covariance with \(y\) equal to its own variance, so conditioning on \(y\) splits the surprise in proportion to the variances:

$$ \mathbb E[\,\text{term} \mid y\,] = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\text{term})}{S}\,(y - m_t^-), \qquad \frac{P_t^-}{S} + \frac{\sigma_a^2}{S} + \frac{\tau^2/D}{S} + \frac{1/I}{S} = 1 . $$

The core share is \(K\). The set share gives the estimate of this set-format's deviation:

$$ \hat a_t = \frac{\sigma_a^2}{S}\,(y - m_t^-), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(a_t \mid y) = \sigma_a^2 - \frac{\sigma_a^4}{S} . $$

Only the core part carries forward. The set, deck and luck parts are written off when the next set-format starts.

7. Assigning skill groups

Groups for a new set-format are fixed before it starts. Each player is classified from their carried-forward belief \(\mathcal N(m^-, P^-)\): Step 1 applied up to the new set's start, using none of its results. Two consequences follow:

  • A player's group reflects every earlier set-format, weighted as the filter weights them, not just the last three.
  • Time away matters. The drift added since a player's last set-format widens \(P^-\), which can drop them out of a group without their rating moving.

The bands keep their current definitions: bottom 0–25%, middle 33–67% and top 75–100% of players with 50+ games. With uncertain ratings, the band edges are quantiles of the population's skill distribution. That distribution is estimated as the mixture of carried-forward beliefs of the \(N\) reference players, those with 50+ games in the most recent set-format:

$$ F(z) = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^{N} \Phi\!\left(\frac{z - m_i^-}{\sqrt{P_i^-}}\right), \qquad F(\ell_g) = \text{lower percentile of band } g, \quad F(u_g) = \text{upper percentile}. $$

A player's probability of being in band \(g\), and the assignment rule, are

$$ \pi_g = \Phi\!\left(\frac{u_g - m^-}{\sqrt{P^-}}\right) - \Phi\!\left(\frac{\ell_g - m^-}{\sqrt{P^-}}\right), \qquad \text{assign to } g \text{ if } \pi_g \ge q . $$

The threshold \(q\) must exceed 0.5 so no one qualifies for two bands. It is 0.8 by default and will be chosen by backtesting precision against the current rule. A player who clears no threshold is left unassigned, as players outside the bands are today.

Only core skill is classified. The coming set's deviation \(a\) is unknown when groups are fixed, and it's the part a group label shouldn't predict: groups describe players, not their next set's luck.

9. Approximations and caveats

  • Deck noise is aggregated. Treating \(\bar e_t\) as one shift on the whole set-format ignores how deck quality interacts with Premier Draft's 7-win / 3-loss stopping rule. The per-draft record moments (draft_win_sq_sum and friends) are there to estimate \(\tau\) directly.
  • The cap is a belief rule. A random walk has no stationary spread. Capping \(P\) at \(\sigma^2\) is coherent for the prior a returning player gets, not for a generative model.
  • Linearization moves the state; quadrature scores it. The quadratic approximation behind each Newton step is accurate when \(I\) is large. For a handful of games the posterior is skewed and the Gaussian state is an approximation. The likelihood used for fitting doesn't rely on it.
  • Cube is not a set. Run as one more set-format in a Premier Draft chain, cube events inflate the set deviation: in the five-player data \(\hat\sigma_a\) is about 0.10 with cube events and 0.07 without. Cube probably needs its own chain, or exclusion.
  • Rank is the player's own rank. \(\beta_r\) adjusts for the strength of the pool a player is matched into at that rank, not for a particular opponent's rank.
  • Example parameters are provisional. \(c\), \(\sigma_a\) and \(\beta_r\) come from five highly engaged community players. \(\sigma\) is an earlier estimate, \(\mu_0\) comes from public data, \(\tau\) is set from the event records, and set baselines are zero. The numbers show the mechanics, not production values.

Appendix A. The update as Newton's method

Readers who know Newton's method for logistic regression already know this update. It is one regularized Newton step, with the regularization coming from the previous set-format.

A.1 Newton on the log posterior

For one player and one set-format, combine the record's log-likelihood \(\ell(x)\) (Section 4) with the carried-forward prior \(\mathcal N(m^-, P^-)\). Up to a constant, the log posterior is

$$ J(x) = \ell(x) - \frac{(x - m^-)^2}{2P^-} . $$

Its first derivative is the score minus the prior's pull, and its negative second derivative is the information plus the prior's precision:

$$ J'(x) = U(x) - \frac{x - m^-}{P^-}, \qquad -J''(x) = I(x) + \frac{1}{P^-} . $$

A Newton step from \(\hat x\) is \(\hat x + J'(\hat x) / \big({-J''(\hat x)}\big)\):

$$ x_{\text{new}} = \hat x + \frac{U(\hat x) - (\hat x - m^-)/P^-}{I(\hat x) + 1/P^-} . $$

This is ridge (MAP) logistic regression with one coefficient, \(x\), and the benchmark \(o_r\) as a fixed offset. A ridge penalty \(\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\alpha^2\) is the special case \(m^- = 0\), \(1/P^- = \lambda\): a Gaussian prior centred at zero. Here the prior is centred on the player's previous rating, and its strength \(1/P^-\) is whatever the filter has learned, loosened by drift since the last set-format.

A.2 The Kalman step is that Newton step

Leave out set deviation and deck noise for the moment, so \(R = 1/I\). Start from the Kalman update of Section 5 with \(y = \hat x + U/I\):

$$ \begin{aligned} m &= m^- + \frac{P^-}{P^- + 1/I}\,(y - m^-) \\ &= \frac{m^-/P^- + I\,y}{1/P^- + I} \\ &= \frac{m^-/P^- + I\,\hat x + U}{1/P^- + I} \\ &= \hat x + \frac{U - (\hat x - m^-)/P^-}{I + 1/P^-} \end{aligned} $$
  1. The Kalman update with \(K = P^-/(P^- + R)\).
  2. Multiply through by \(I/P^-\): the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of prior and observation.
  3. Substitute \(y = \hat x + U/I\).
  4. Subtract \(\hat x\) from the numerator. The result is the Newton step of A.1.

The variance matches too. \(P = (1-K)P^- = 1/(1/P^- + I)\) is the inverse of \(-J''\): the Laplace approximation, which reads the posterior variance off the curvature at the mode. Relinearizing at the new mean and repeating the step from the same prior is just iterating Newton to the posterior mode. That is Bell and Cathey's (1993) result, and why the passes in Section 8 settle at once.

A.3 What set deviation and deck noise change

With them, \(R = 1/I + v\) where \(v = \sigma_a^2 + \tau^2/D\). The record measures effective skill \(x + a + \bar e\). Its Gaussian approximation \(\mathcal N(y;\, x,\, 1/I)\), convolved with the \(\mathcal N(0, v)\) noise, gives \(\mathcal N(y;\, x,\, 1/I + v)\): Gaussian variances add. Running the algebra of A.2 with this \(R\) gives the same Newton step with tempered score and information:

$$ x_{\text{new}} = \hat x + \frac{\tilde U - (\hat x - m^-)/P^-}{\tilde I + 1/P^-}, \qquad \tilde U = \frac{U}{1 + I v}, \quad \tilde I = \frac{I}{1 + I v} = \frac{1}{R} . $$

Every game counts for less by the same factor \(1/(1 + Iv)\). The factor shrinks as the record grows, so a set-format's information can never exceed \(1/v \approx 1/\sigma_a^2\). One set is one draw of the set deviation, and more games don't average it away. In the OTJ example, \(I = 83.9\) and \(v = 0.075^2 + 0.10^2/49 = 0.00583\), so

$$ \tilde I = \frac{83.9}{1 + 83.9 \times 0.00583} = 56.3, \qquad \frac{1}{v} = 172 . $$

OTJ counts about two-thirds as much as its 362 games would without set deviation. At convergence the tempered score balances the prior's pull, \(\tilde U = 13.59/1.489 = 9.13 \approx (0.5359 - 0.4092)/0.01388 = 9.13\), and the posterior variance is the inverse curvature, \(P = 1/(56.3 + 72.0) = 0.00779\), as in Section 8.

A.4 More coefficients

With many coefficients, as in a regression on every card in a set, the same step holds with \(U\) a gradient vector, \(I + 1/P^-\) a Hessian matrix, and division replaced by a linear solve. The binomial is the two-outcome case of the softmax (conditional logit). For more outcomes, \(p(1-p)\) becomes \(\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top\). The rating update only stays a scalar because each player has one skill, and players are independent given the benchmark.
